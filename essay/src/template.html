02 / FOLLOW THE ADDITION

The powers kept growing.
Their digits overlapped.

Because 997 = 1000 − 3, its reciprocal is a geometric series:

1997= 11000+ 31000²+ 91000³+…

Each numerator triples while its place moves three decimal digits to the right. The amounts get smaller, and we add them. Once a power needs more room, its digits reach back into earlier groups. Check the identity.

The progression continues forever. To find the repeating part, we can keep much less information: just the leftover from each division.

03 / REMEMBER THE REMAINDER

The leftover remembers
what comes next.

The powers grow without end. To print the next group, long division needs only its remainder: the whole-number leftover from the previous step. For 1/997 it always stays between 0 and 996.

How the remainder loop also reads in base 3

Look at the small counts from splitting three times a remainder: each is 0, 1, or 2. Those are also the digits of 1/997 in base 3. The registered digit-periodicity result supplies the division rule; the two-readout identity below is a specialized algebraic derivation.

AT THE SELECTED GROUP

3 × 729 = 2 × 997 + 193

Base-3 digit: 2 2 whole copies of 997

Three-digit decimal word: 729 + 2 = 731

Set ri = 3i mod 997 and di = floor(3ri/997). Then:

3rᵢ = 997dᵢ + rᵢ₊₁
1000rᵢ = 997(rᵢ + dᵢ) + rᵢ₊₁
Wᵢ = rᵢ + dᵢ

The first line prints a base-3 digit. The second prints a three-digit decimal word. Both pass along the same remainder, so they share the 166-step loop. The local digit di is distinct from the unbounded carry of the full powers.

729

For example, the full power decomposes as 3i = 997qi + ri, where qi = floor(3i/997). Discarding qi leaves the bounded state that drives the next digits.

Sources, scope, and further reading

Recurrence generating functions and reciprocal digit patterns have a substantial history. These primary references place the examples in that context:

  • C. T. Long (1981), “The Decimal Expansion of 1/89 and Related Results” — Fibonacci expansions and finite tails.
  • G. Köhler (1985), “Generating Functions of Fibonacci-Like Sequences and Decimal Expansions of Some Fractions” — recurrence-based expansions.
  • Jin-Zai Lee and Jia-Sheng Lee (1987), “A Complete Characterization of B-Power Fractions That Can Be Represented as Series of General n-Bonacci Numbers” — related recurrence constructions.
  • Marjorie Bicknell-Johnson (1989), “Retrograde Renegades and the Pascal Connection: Repeating Decimals Represented by Fibonacci and Other Sequences Appearing from Right to Left” — recurrence sequences read from the right of repeating decimals.

Every interactive step uses exact integer arithmetic and is checked against ordinary long division. These checks support the implementation; the identities above provide the mathematical argument. The page does not claim that the full recurrence framework has been formally verified in Lean.

A NOTE FROM MIKE

A small surprise worth following.

I started by adding nines and watching the powers stay visible longer. The small surprise—729 becoming 731—led from a curious decimal to carrying, remainders, and the question of when we know enough. This essay is an invitation to follow that curiosity.
